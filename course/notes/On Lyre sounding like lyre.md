# On Lyre sounding like lyre

A lyre’s tone begins with a burst of energy and quickly settles into something soft and resonant. Its **ADSR envelope** is short and asymmetric — **attack** almost instant, **decay** fast, **sustain** gently fading, and **release** natural but brief. The sound doesn’t swell like a wind instrument; it appears suddenly, glows for a moment, and then drifts away.

When you pluck a string, the amplitude reaches its peak within 5–20 ms, then falls to roughly half in a few hundred milliseconds. After that, the sound keeps diminishing as the energy dissipates through the string and body. The release phase depends on how the player stops the note — if muted, it ends abruptly; if left to ring, the resonance may last close to a second. The pitch stays essentially stable throughout, with only a microscopic rise during the pluck as tension spikes. What really changes is the **brightness**: high harmonics dominate at the start and fade much faster than the lower ones, turning a sharp, metallic opening into a mellow, woody tail.

---

## What makes a lyre sound like a lyre

* **Harmonic richness:** It isn’t a sine wave — it’s full of overtones that decay unevenly.
* **Pluck transient:** The attack contains a short, bright noise or high-frequency burst that defines the string being struck.
* **Fast envelope:** Short attack and decay; no steady plateau.
* **Evolving brightness:** Treble fades first, leaving a warm low-mid resonance.
* **Natural body resonance:** A soft bump around 200–400 Hz gives the lyre its hollow, harp-like tone.

---

If your PCM output sounds like a flute, it’s because it’s too clean — a flute has a smooth sine-like waveform and long, even sustain. To evoke a lyre, start with a richer waveform (triangle, saw, or a filtered noise burst), keep the attack short, and let both the loudness and the high frequencies decay together. The result should feel plucked rather than blown — bright at first, then quickly relaxing into a gentle shimmer.
